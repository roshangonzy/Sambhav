Acids, Bases & Salts

The Ultimate Deep-Dive for ICSE Grade 10 • Sambhav

Module 1: The World of Acids

The term 'acid' comes from the Latin word acidus, meaning sour. But in Grade 10, physical properties are not enough. We must define acids strictly by their molecular behavior in water (Arrhenius Theory).

1.1 The Strict ICSE Definition

An acid is a compound which when dissolved in water yields Hydronium ions ($\ce{H3O+}$) as the only positively charged ions.

The Chemistry Behind It: Pure acids (like $\ce{HCl}$ gas or glacial $\ce{H2SO4}$) are covalent compounds. They do not contain ions. However, when added to water, the polar water molecules force them to undergo ionization. The acid releases a bare proton ($\ce{H+}$). Because a bare proton is incredibly unstable, it instantly bonds with the lone pair of a water molecule via a coordinate bond to form the Hydronium ion.

$ \ce{HCl + H2O -> H3O+ + Cl-} $

$ \text{(Or simply: } \ce{HCl -> H+ + Cl-} \text{)} $
Crucial Keyword Trap

The Word "Only"

If you define an acid as "a compound that produces Hydronium ions in water," you will lose marks. You MUST write "as the only positively charged ions." For example, Sodium Bisulphate ($\ce{NaHSO4}$) produces $\ce{H3O+}$, but it also produces $\ce{Na+}$. Therefore, it is NOT a pure acid; it is an acid salt.

1.2 Classification of Acids

Acids are classified in several different ways. You must know all of them to handle complex reasoning questions.

A. Depending on Source:

  • Organic Acids: Found in plants and animals. They contain Carbon. They are naturally weak acids (do not ionize completely). Examples: Acetic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$), Citric acid, Oxalic acid.
  • Inorganic (Mineral) Acids: Derived from minerals in the earth. They do not contain Carbon (with the exception of Carbonic Acid $\ce{H2CO3}$). They are usually strong acids. Examples: $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{H2SO4}$.

B. Depending on Strength (Degree of Ionization $\alpha$):

  • Strong Acids: Ionize almost completely ($>30\%$) in aqueous solutions. They contain a high concentration of hydronium ions and only ions. Example: $\ce{HCl (aq) -> H+ + Cl-}$.
  • Weak Acids: Ionize only partially ($<5\%$) in aqueous solutions. They contain a low concentration of hydronium ions and a mixture of ions AND un-ionized molecules. Example: $\ce{CH3COOH \rightleftharpoons CH3COO- + H+}$.

C. Depending on Basicity (EXTREMELY IMPORTANT):

Basicity is defined as the number of ionizable hydrogen atoms ($\ce{H+}$) present in one molecule of an acid.

The Acetic Acid Paradox

Acetic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$) clearly contains 4 Hydrogen atoms. Why is its basicity only 1?

Answer: Basicity relies only on ionizable hydrogens. In acetic acid, the three hydrogens attached to the Carbon atom are held by strong, non-polar covalent bonds and cannot be broken by water. Only the single Hydrogen attached to the highly electronegative Oxygen atom ($-\ce{COOH}$ group) can break off as an $\ce{H+}$ ion.

Check Your Grip: Acids

1. Explain why pure anhydrous $\ce{HCl}$ gas does not change the color of dry blue litmus paper, but dilute $\ce{HCl}$ does.

Reveal Answer
Answer: Pure anhydrous $\ce{HCl}$ is a covalent molecule and contains no free ions. The acidic property (turning litmus red) is caused only by the presence of Hydronium ions ($\ce{H3O+}$). Dilute $\ce{HCl}$ contains water, which forces the $\ce{HCl}$ to ionize and produce these $\ce{H3O+}$ ions.

2. Name a dibasic, weak, inorganic acid.

Reveal Answer
Answer: Carbonic Acid ($\ce{H2CO3}$) or Sulphurous Acid ($\ce{H2SO3}$).

Module 2: Bases & Alkalis

Acids and Bases are chemical opposites. While acids produce $\ce{H+}$, bases exist to neutralize them.

2.1 The Definition of a Base

A Base is defined as either a metallic oxide (e.g., $\ce{CuO}$, $\ce{MgO}$) or a metallic hydroxide (e.g., $\ce{Zn(OH)2}$, $\ce{Pb(OH)2}$) which reacts with an acid to form salt and water only. This process is called Neutralization.

$ \ce{CuO + 2HCl -> CuCl2 + H2O} $

2.2 The Definition of an Alkali

An Alkali is simply a water-soluble base. Because it dissolves in water, we can define it using Arrhenius theory: An alkali is a compound which when dissolved in water yields Hydroxyl ions ($\ce{OH-}$ ) as the only negatively charged ions.

Golden Rule: All alkalis are bases, but all bases are NOT alkalis.

Examples of Alkalis: Sodium Hydroxide ($\ce{NaOH}$), Potassium Hydroxide ($\ce{KOH}$), Calcium Hydroxide ($\ce{Ca(OH)2}$), and Ammonium Hydroxide ($\ce{NH4OH}$ - the only non-metallic alkali).

2.3 Classification by Acidity

Just as acids have "Basicity", bases have "Acidity". Acidity of a base is the number of replaceable hydroxyl ($\ce{OH-}$) ions present in one molecule.

  • Monoacidic Base (Acidity = 1): Yields one $\ce{OH-}$ ion. E.g., $\ce{NaOH}$, $\ce{KOH}$, $\ce{NH4OH}$.
  • Diacidic Base (Acidity = 2): Yields two $\ce{OH-}$ ions. E.g., $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$, $\ce{Cu(OH)2}$.
  • Triacidic Base (Acidity = 3): Yields three $\ce{OH-}$ ions. E.g., $\ce{Al(OH)3}$, $\ce{Fe(OH)3}$.

Check Your Grip: Bases

1. Lead oxide ($\ce{PbO}$) reacts with Hydrochloric acid to form Lead chloride and water. Can we classify $\ce{PbO}$ as an alkali? Give a reason.

Reveal Answer
Answer: No, $\ce{PbO}$ is NOT an alkali. While it acts as a base by neutralizing the acid, it is insoluble in water. Alkalis are strictly defined as bases that dissolve in water to produce hydroxyl ions.

Module 3: Indicators & The pH Scale

We cannot taste chemicals to determine if they are acidic or alkaline. We use chemical dyes called Indicators, which undergo a structural change and alter their color depending on whether they are exposed to Hydronium ions ($\ce{H3O+}$) or Hydroxyl ions ($\ce{OH-}$).

3.2 The pH Scale & Universal Indicator

Standard indicators tell you if a substance is an acid or an alkali, but not how strong it is. In 1909, Sorensen devised the pH scale (power of Hydrogen) to measure the exact concentration of Hydrogen/Hydronium ions in a solution. Mathematically, $pH = -\log[H^+]$. The scale runs from 0 to 14.

  • pH = 7: Neutral (Pure distilled water). Concentration of $\ce{H^+}$ exactly equals the concentration of $\ce{OH-}$.
  • pH < 7: Acidic. The closer to 0, the stronger the acid. ($\ce{H+}$ dominates).
  • pH > 7: Alkaline/Basic. The closer to 14, the stronger the alkali. ($\ce{OH-}$ dominates).

To measure this visually, we use a Universal Indicator or pH paper. It is a mixture of several chemical dyes that shows a specific, distinct color for every pH value, mimicking the colors of the rainbow (VIBGYOR):

Check Your Grip: pH

1. Solution A has a pH of 3. Solution B has a pH of 5. Which solution has a higher concentration of Hydrogen ions?

Reveal Answer
Answer: Solution A. On the pH scale, lower values indicate a much higher concentration of $\ce{H+}$ ions (a stronger acid).

Module 4: Salts & Their Types

When an acid reacts with a base, they destroy each other's properties to form water. What is left behind is an ionic compound called a Salt.

Definition Focus

The Formal Definition

A salt is a chemical compound formed by the partial or complete replacement of the ionizable hydrogen atoms of an acid by a metallic ion (like $\ce{Na+}$) or an ammonium ion ($\ce{NH4+}$).

4.1 The Step-by-Step Formation of Salts

Let's observe Sulphuric Acid ($\ce{H2SO4}$). It is a dibasic acid, meaning it has TWO replaceable hydrogens. It can form two entirely different types of salts depending on how much base ($\ce{NaOH}$) we supply.

Type 1: Acid Salts (Partial Replacement)

If we supply an insufficient amount of base, only ONE hydrogen atom is replaced. The resulting salt still contains an ionizable hydrogen. If you dissolve it in water, it will yield $\ce{H+}$ ions and turn blue litmus red!

$ \ce{NaOH + H2SO4 -> NaHSO4 + H2O} $

Forms Sodium Hydrogen Sulphate (Sodium Bisulphate).

Other Examples: Sodium bicarbonate ($\ce{NaHCO3}$), Potassium bisulphite ($\ce{KHSO3}$).

Type 2: Normal Salts (Complete Replacement)

If we supply excess or sufficient base, BOTH hydrogen atoms are replaced. The salt contains NO replaceable hydrogen. Their aqueous solutions are generally neutral to litmus.

$ \ce{2NaOH + H2SO4 -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O} $

Forms Sodium Sulphate.

Other Examples: $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{CaCO3}$, $\ce{CuSO4}$.

Type 3: Basic Salts

Formed by the incomplete neutralization of a polyacidic base. They still contain a replaceable hydroxyl ($\ce{OH-}$) group in their molecule.

$ \ce{Pb(OH)2 + HCl -> Pb(OH)Cl + H2O} $

Forms Basic lead chloride.

Other Examples: Basic copper chloride ($\ce{Cu(OH)Cl}$), Basic lead nitrate ($\ce{Pb(OH)NO3}$).

Type 4: Complex Salts

These salts contain a simple ion outside brackets and a "complex ion" inside square brackets. When dissolved in water, the complex ion does NOT break apart into simpler ions. It remains a single entity.

$ \ce{Na[Ag(CN)2] \rightleftharpoons Na+ + [Ag(CN)2]-} $

Examples: Sodium argentocyanide $\ce{Na[Ag(CN)2]}$, Potassium ferrocyanide $\ce{K4[Fe(CN)6]}$.

ICSE 2018

Past Year Question

Define an acid salt and give one example.

Reveal Official Board Answer
An acid salt is a salt formed by the partial replacement of the replaceable hydrogen atoms of a polybasic acid by a metallic or ammonium ion. It still contains replaceable hydrogen.

Example: Sodium hydrogen sulphate ($\ce{NaHSO4}$).

Module 6: Salt Preparation (Master Guide)

The ICSE syllabus requires you to know six specific chemical methods to prepare normal salts. However, before you can choose a method to prepare a salt, you MUST know if the salt you are trying to make dissolves in water (Soluble) or not (Insoluble).

The Solubility Cheat Sheet (Memorize!)

SOLUBLE SALTS

  • ALL salts of Sodium ($\ce{Na}$), Potassium ($\ce{K}$), and Ammonium ($\ce{NH4}$).
  • ALL Nitrates ($\ce{NO3}$).
  • ALL Chlorides/Bromides...
    (Exception: Silver, Lead, Mercury).
  • ALL Sulphates...
    (Exception: Barium, Lead, Calcium).

INSOLUBLE SALTS (Precipitates)

  • ALL Carbonates...
    (Exception: Na, K, NH4).
  • ALL Sulphites & Sulphides...
    (Exception: Na, K, NH4).

The 6 Preparation Methods

1. Direct Combination (Synthesis)

Heating a metal directly with a non-metal. Used primarily to make binary salts like Sulphides and Chlorides.

$ \ce{Fe + S ->[\Delta] FeS} $
Iron + Sulphur $\rightarrow$ Iron(II) Sulphide

2. Simple Displacement

An active metal (must be above Hydrogen in the reactivity series, like Mg, Zn, Fe) reacts with a dilute acid to displace hydrogen gas.

$ \ce{Zn + H2SO4 (dil) -> ZnSO4 + H2 \uparrow} $

3. Precipitation (Double Decomposition) — For Insoluble Salts

If the salt you want to make is insoluble (e.g., Barium Sulphate or Lead Chloride), you MUST use this method. You mix two soluble solutions together. They swap ions, forming the insoluble salt which falls to the bottom as a precipitate ($\downarrow$), allowing you to easily filter it out.

$ \ce{BaCl2 (aq) + H2SO4 (aq) -> BaSO4 \downarrow + 2HCl} $
Barium Sulphate forms as a white precipitate.

4. Neutralization of Insoluble Base

Used for soluble salts of metals like Copper, Lead, or Zinc. You take a solid, insoluble oxide/hydroxide and warm it with dilute acid until it dissolves. Filter the excess solid, and evaporate the liquid to get crystals.

$ \ce{CuO (s) + H2SO4 (dil) ->[\Delta] CuSO4 (aq) + H2O} $

5. Neutralization of an Alkali (Titration) — For Na, K, NH4 Salts

If you want to make a soluble salt of Na, K, or NH4, you cannot use Method 4. Why? Because their bases are Alkalis (water-soluble). Since both the acid and the alkali are clear liquids, and the resulting salt is soluble, you can't just filter out the excess! You must use a burette and an indicator (Titration) to find the exact neutralization point drop-by-drop.

$ \ce{NaOH (aq) + HCl (aq) -> NaCl (aq) + H2O} $

6. Action of Acid on Carbonates

Reacting a carbonate or bicarbonate with dilute acid to form salt, water, and CO₂ gas.

$ \ce{CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2 \uparrow} $
ICSE 2021

Past Year Question

Name the method used for the preparation of the following salts:
(i) Barium sulphate
(ii) Sodium sulphate.

Reveal Official Board Answer
(i) Barium Sulphate is an insoluble salt (see solubility rules). Therefore, it must be prepared by Precipitation (Double Decomposition).

(ii) Sodium Sulphate is a soluble salt of an alkali metal. Therefore, it must be prepared by Neutralization of an alkali (Titration) using Sodium Hydroxide and Sulphuric acid.
