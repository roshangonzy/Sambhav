Chemical Bonding

The Ultimate ICSE Grade 10 Comprehensive Guide

Part 0: Why Do Atoms Bond?

Welcome to the foundation of Chemistry, Sambhav. If you want to understand how the entire universe is constructed, you must first understand why atoms combine. In the universe, everything strives for a state of minimum energy and maximum stability.

For atoms, true stability means having a fully packed outermost electron shell (the valence shell). When an atom's outermost shell is full, it becomes highly unreactive and stable. We look to the Noble Gases (Group 18 of the periodic table, like Helium, Neon, and Argon) as the gold standard of atomic stability because they naturally possess full outer shells.

  • The Duplet Rule: Achieving exactly 2 electrons in the outermost shell. This rule primarily applies to elements very close to Helium (like Hydrogen and Lithium), which only have a single K-shell that maxes out at 2 electrons.
  • The Octet Rule: Achieving exactly 8 electrons in the outermost shell. This applies to almost all other elements (like Oxygen, Carbon, Chlorine). Having 8 valence electrons makes them stable like Neon or Argon.

Because most atoms in nature do not naturally have 8 valence electrons (they might have 1, or 4, or 7), they are inherently unstable and agitated. To achieve this "Noble Gas Configuration", atoms are forced to interact with each other. To get those magic 8 electrons, they will either lose, gain, or share electrons. This interaction, this "glue" that forms between them as a result of electron movement, is what we call a Chemical Bond.

Definition Focus

Chemical Bond

A chemical bond is defined as the force of attraction that holds two or more atoms together as a single entity (forming a molecule or an ionic compound) to achieve a stable electronic configuration.

Quick Check: Fundamentals

1. Why are Noble Gases chemically inert (unreactive)?

View Answer
Noble gases already possess a stable electronic configuration. Their outermost shell is completely filled (satisfying either the duplet rule for Helium or the octet rule for the rest), meaning they do not need to lose, gain, or share electrons.

2. What are the three primary ways an unstable atom can achieve an octet?

View Answer
An atom can achieve a stable octet by:
1. Losing one or more electrons.
2. Gaining one or more electrons.
3. Mutually sharing one or more pairs of electrons with another atom.

Part 1: Electrovalent (Ionic) Bonding

Electrovalent bonding (also known as Ionic bonding) is the chemistry of extremes. It occurs exclusively between a Metal (which has 1, 2, or 3 valence electrons) and a Non-Metal (which has 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons).

Because the metal desperately wants to get rid of its extra electrons to reveal the full shell underneath, and the non-metal desperately wants to grab extra electrons to fill its current shell, a complete transfer of one or more valence electrons takes place.

  • The Metal Atom: Loses electrons. In chemistry, the loss of electrons is called Oxidation. By losing negatively charged electrons, the atom is left with more positive protons than negative electrons. It becomes a positively charged ion, known as a Cation.
  • The Non-Metal Atom: Gains electrons. The gain of electrons is called Reduction. By gaining extra negative charge, it becomes a negatively charged ion, known as an Anion.

Now, according to the fundamental laws of physics, opposite charges attract. The newly formed Cation ($+$) and Anion ($-$) are pulled towards each other by a massive, invisible magnetic-like force. This strong electrostatic force of attraction that locks these ions together is the Ionic Bond.

Note: Electrovalency is the specific number of electrons an atom loses or gains to form this bond. For example, Sodium loses 1 electron, so its electrovalency is $+1$. Oxygen gains 2 electrons, so its electrovalency is $-2$.

Crucial Conditions for Formation of an Ionic Bond:

ICSE frequently asks for these three conditions. You must understand why they are required:

  1. Low Ionisation Potential (IP) of the metal: Ionisation Potential is the energy required to rip an electron away from an atom. For an ionic bond to form easily, the metal must have a low IP, meaning it surrenders its valence electrons willingly without requiring massive amounts of heat or energy.
  2. High Electron Affinity (EA) of the non-metal: Electron Affinity is the energy released when an atom accepts an electron. The non-metal must have a high EA, meaning it aggressively grabs the incoming electron and releases a lot of stabilizing energy in the process.
  3. Large Electronegativity (EN) difference: Electronegativity is an atom's pulling power on electrons. For a complete transfer to happen (instead of sharing), one atom (the non-metal) must be significantly stronger at pulling electrons than the other (the metal).
ICSE Board Trap

The Brackets & Charges Rule

When asked to draw the electron dot structure (Lewis structure) for an electrovalent compound, you MUST use square brackets $[ \ ]$ around the final products and write the net charge ($+, -, 2+, 2-$) outside the top right corner. The non-metal must show all 8 electrons inside its bracket. If you draw the dots but forget the brackets and charges, examiners will give you ZERO marks, because without charges, it's not an ion!

Deep Dive: The 3 Standard ICSE Examples

Example 1: Formation of Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

Sodium (Na, Atomic No. 11) has an electronic configuration of $2,8,1$. Chlorine (Cl, Atomic No. 17) has an electronic configuration of $2,8,7$.

Sodium is unstable with its 1 extra valence electron. Chlorine is frustrated because it is just 1 electron short of an octet. Sodium undergoes oxidation, transferring its 1 electron entirely to Chlorine. Sodium becomes $Na^+$ (now a stable $2,8$), and Chlorine becomes $Cl^-$ (now a stable $2,8,8$).

$$ \ce{Na (2,8,1) -> Na^+ (2,8) + 1e^-} $$ $$ \ce{Cl (2,8,7) + 1e^- -> Cl^- (2,8,8)} $$
Example 2: Formation of Magnesium Chloride (MgCl₂)

Magnesium (Mg, Atomic No. 12) has a configuration of $2,8,2$. It must lose 2 electrons to achieve stability. However, a single Chlorine atom ($2,8,7$) only has room to accept 1 electron.

The solution? One Magnesium atom invites two separate Chlorine atoms to the reaction. Magnesium gives 1 electron to the first Chlorine, and 1 electron to the second Chlorine. Magnesium becomes $Mg^{2+}$, and two separate Chloride ions ($Cl^-$) are formed.

$$ \ce{Mg (2,8,2) -> Mg^{2+} (2,8) + 2e^-} $$ $$ \ce{2Cl (2,8,7) + 2e^- -> 2Cl^- (2,8,8)} $$
Example 3: Formation of Calcium Oxide (CaO)

Calcium (Ca, Atomic No. 20) is $2,8,8,2$. It needs to lose 2 electrons. Oxygen (O, Atomic No. 8) is $2,6$. It conveniently needs exactly 2 electrons to reach an octet.

A direct, complete transfer occurs. Calcium transfers both of its valence electrons directly to a single Oxygen atom. Calcium becomes $Ca^{2+}$, and Oxygen becomes an Oxide ion, $O^{2-}$. Because the charges are double that of NaCl, the electrostatic pull holding CaO together is incredibly strong.

$$ \ce{Ca (2,8,8,2) -> Ca^{2+} (2,8,8) + 2e^-} $$ $$ \ce{O (2,6) + 2e^- -> O^{2-} (2,8)} $$
ICSE 2017

Past Year Question

An element X has atomic number 19. An element Y has atomic number 8. What is the formula of the compound formed between X and Y? What type of bond is formed? [2 Marks]

Show Answer
Step 1: Config of X (Atomic No. 19) is 2,8,8,1. It has 1 valence electron, so it is a metal with an electrovalency of +1.
Step 2: Config of Y (Atomic No. 8) is 2,6. It needs 2 electrons, so it is a non-metal with an electrovalency of -2.
Formula: By criss-crossing the valencies ($X^{+1}$ and $Y^{-2}$), the formula is X₂Y.
Bond Type: Since the bond is between a metal and a non-metal involving electron transfer, it is an Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond.

Quick Check: Electrovalent Bonding

1. If element 'A' is in Group 2 and element 'B' is in Group 17, what kind of bond will they form, and what will be the formula?

View Answer
They will form an Electrovalent (Ionic) bond because Group 2 elements are metals and Group 17 elements are non-metals.
Group 2 means 'A' has a valency of +2. Group 17 means 'B' has a valency of -1. Criss-crossing gives the formula AB₂.

2. Why does the formation of an ionic bond require a large electronegativity difference?

View Answer
A large electronegativity difference ensures that the non-metal's pull on electrons is so overwhelmingly stronger than the metal's hold on them, that the electron is completely ripped away (transferred) rather than being mutually shared.

Part 2: Covalent Bonding

Covalent bonding is the chemistry of compromise. It occurs strictly between two Non-Metals.

Why sharing? Because non-metals (found on the right side of the periodic table) have high Ionization Potentials and high Electron Affinities. This means neither atom is willing to give up an electron, and both are aggressively trying to gain one. Since a complete transfer is impossible, they compromise by mutually sharing one or more electron pairs. The shared pairs orbit both nuclei, belonging to the outermost shells of both atoms simultaneously, counting towards both of their octets.

  • Single Covalent Bond (—): 1 pair (2 electrons) is shared. (e.g., $H_2$, $Cl_2$, $CH_4$)
  • Double Covalent Bond (=): 2 pairs (4 electrons) are shared. (e.g., $O_2$, $C_2H_4$)
  • Triple Covalent Bond ($\equiv$): 3 pairs (6 electrons) are shared. (e.g., $N_2$)

Covalency is the exact number of electron pairs an atom shares with other atoms to achieve stability.

Board Exam Hack

Beware of Lone Pairs!

In covalent bonding, do NOT use square brackets or charges. However, examiners specifically look for Lone Pairs. A lone pair is a pair of valence electrons that do NOT participate in bonding. For example, Nitrogen in $NH_3$ has 5 valence electrons: it uses 3 to bond with Hydrogen, leaving 2 electrons (1 pair) completely unused. This is the lone pair. If you draw the molecule without showing the unused dots, you will lose marks.

A. Non-Polar Covalent Bonds

Non-polar bonds occur between atoms of the same element (like two Chlorine atoms) or elements with a negligible difference in electronegativity (like Carbon and Hydrogen). Because both atoms pull on the shared electrons with equal or nearly equal strength, the shared pair of electrons remains exactly in the center between the two nuclei. No fractional charges (no "poles") are formed. The molecule is perfectly balanced.

Example 1: Hydrogen Molecule ($H_2$)

Two Hydrogen atoms, each with 1 electron, approach each other. They share their single electrons. The single shared pair orbits both atoms, allowing both to satisfy the duplet rule (imitating Helium).

$$ \ce{H \cdot + \cdot H -> H : H -> H - H} $$
One shared pair. Zero lone pairs. Perfectly Non-Polar.
Example 2: Chlorine Molecule ($Cl_2$)

Each Chlorine atom ($2,8,7$) is desperate for 1 electron. By mutually sharing one pair in the middle, both achieve 8 valence electrons. Since they are identical atoms, they pull equally.

$$ \ce{Cl - Cl} $$
Each Chlorine atom is left with 3 lone pairs (6 unshared electrons) around its perimeter.
Example 3: Nitrogen Molecule ($N_2$)

Nitrogen ($2,5$) needs a massive 3 electrons to complete its octet. To solve this, two Nitrogen atoms must share 3 pairs of electrons between them, forming a dense, incredibly strong triple bond.

$$ \ce{:N \equiv N:} $$
Each Nitrogen atom has used 3 electrons for bonding, leaving exactly 1 lone pair on each atom.
Example 4: Methane ($CH_4$) & Carbon Tetrachloride ($CCl_4$)

Carbon ($2,4$) needs 4 electrons. It forms 4 individual single covalent bonds with 4 Hydrogen atoms (or 4 Chlorine atoms). Because Carbon is surrounded symmetrically in a 3D tetrahedral shape, any slight pulls cancel out. The entire molecule remains fundamentally non-polar.

B. Polar Covalent Bonds

Polar bonds occur between dissimilar non-metals with a noticeable difference in electronegativity. Imagine a tug-of-war where one person is a bodybuilder and the other is a child. The more electronegative atom acts like the bodybuilder—it pulls the shared electron pair much closer to its own nucleus.

Because electrons are negatively charged, the atom hoarding the electrons develops a slight negative fractional charge, written as $\delta-$ (delta negative). The atom losing the tug-of-war is slightly stripped of its electron shield, developing a slight positive fractional charge, written as $\delta+$ (delta positive). The molecule now has a positive end and a negative end. It has developed "poles."

Example 5: Water ($H_2O$)

Oxygen ($2,6$) needs 2 electrons. It shares 1 electron with each of two Hydrogen atoms. Oxygen is vastly more electronegative than Hydrogen. It pulls the shared electron pairs greedily towards itself. The Oxygen atom becomes $\delta-$, and the two Hydrogens become $\delta+$.

Furthermore, Oxygen has 6 valence electrons but only used 2 for bonding. This leaves it with 2 lone pairs sitting on top, pushing the Hydrogen atoms down into an asymmetrical "bent" shape, intensifying the polarity.

Example 6: Ammonia ($NH_3$)

Nitrogen ($2,5$) forms 3 single bonds with 3 Hydrogen atoms. Nitrogen is the more electronegative bully, becoming $\delta-$. The molecule has 3 bond pairs and exactly 1 lone pair resting on the Nitrogen atom, creating a pyramidal, polar shape.

ICSE 2023

Past Year Question

Explain why Hydrogen Chloride (HCl) gas is a polar covalent compound. [2 Marks]

Show Answer
In the HCl molecule, Chlorine is significantly more electronegative than Hydrogen. Therefore, Chlorine exerts a stronger pull and attracts the mutually shared pair of electrons closer to its own nucleus. This unequal sharing of electrons develops a slight fractional negative charge ($\delta-$) on the Chlorine atom and a slight fractional positive charge ($\delta+$) on the Hydrogen atom, resulting in the formation of a polar covalent compound.

Quick Check: Covalent Bonding

1. How many lone pairs and bond pairs of electrons are present in a single molecule of Ammonia ($NH_3$)?

View Answer
There are 3 bond pairs (between N and the 3 H atoms) and 1 lone pair (the 2 unused valence electrons sitting on the Nitrogen atom).

2. Why is Carbon Tetrachloride ($CCl_4$) considered non-polar, even though the C-Cl bonds themselves are polar?

View Answer
$CCl_4$ has a perfectly symmetrical tetrahedral geometric shape. Even though each Chlorine atom pulls electrons away from the central Carbon atom, they pull with equal strength in exactly opposite, symmetrical directions. The polarities cancel each other out entirely, resulting in a net dipole moment of zero.

Part 3: Coordinate (Dative) Bonding

A Coordinate Bond is a highly specialized type of covalent bond. In a standard covalent bond, both interacting atoms bring 1 electron to the table to form a shared pair. It's a 50/50 partnership.

In a coordinate bond, however, the shared pair of electrons is provided entirely by only ONE of the combining atoms. The other atom brings nothing to the table but an empty space.

  • The Donor: The atom that generously provides the entire lone pair of electrons for sharing. To do this, it must have at least one lone pair available (like Nitrogen in $NH_3$ or Oxygen in $H_2O$).
  • The Acceptor: The atom or ion that is completely short of a pair of electrons and happily accepts the share. The most common acceptor in ICSE is the Hydrogen ion ($H^+$), which is essentially just a naked proton with 0 electrons.

The bond is visually represented not by a straight line, but by an arrow ($\rightarrow$) pointing strictly from the donor atom to the acceptor atom. Crucially, once this bond is formed, it is physically identical to and behaves exactly like a normal single covalent bond.

ICSE Standard Example 1: The Hydronium Ion ($H_3O^+$)

When an acid (like HCl) dissolves in water, it releases a Hydrogen ion ($H^+$). This $H^+$ is violently unstable because it has zero electrons.

A nearby water molecule ($H_2O$) has an Oxygen atom possessing two lone pairs. The Oxygen atom takes pity on the $H^+$ ion and donates one of its lone pairs to be shared with the $H^+$. An arrow is drawn from the O to the incoming H. The entire new structure inherits the positive charge of the proton, resulting in the Hydronium ion, $[H_3O]^+$.

$$ \ce{H2O + H^+ -> [H3O]+} $$
ICSE Standard Example 2: The Ammonium Ion ($NH_4^+$)

Similarly, an ammonia molecule ($NH_3$) features a Nitrogen atom harboring exactly one lone pair. When exposed to an acidic environment containing $H^+$ ions, the Nitrogen atom donates its lone pair to a Hydrogen ion.

The resulting structure has 3 normal covalent bonds (N—H) and 1 coordinate bond (N $\rightarrow$ H). Because it absorbed a positive proton, the whole molecule becomes the positively charged Ammonium ion, $[NH_4]^+$.

$$ \ce{NH3 + H^+ -> [NH4]+} $$
Crucial Distinction

The "All-in-One" Molecule

ICSE loves to ask this specific question: "Name a compound that contains all three types of bonds (Electrovalent, Covalent, and Coordinate)." The ultimate answer is Ammonium Chloride ($NH_4Cl$).

1. Covalent bonds exist between the central Nitrogen and 3 Hydrogen atoms to form basic ammonia ($NH_3$).
2. A Coordinate bond exists when Nitrogen donates its lone pair to an $H^+$ to form the Ammonium cation ($NH_4^+$).
3. An Electrovalent (Ionic) bond exists between the massive positive $[NH_4]^+$ cation and the negative Chloride anion $[Cl]^-$!

Quick Check: Coordinate Bonding

1. In the formation of the Hydronium ion, which atom acts as the donor, and what exactly is it donating?

View Answer
The Oxygen atom inside the water ($H_2O$) molecule acts as the donor. It is donating a lone pair of electrons for mutual sharing with the electron-deficient Hydrogen ion ($H^+$).

2. How do you visually differentiate a coordinate bond from a normal covalent bond in a structural diagram?

View Answer
A normal covalent bond is drawn as a straight, solid line (—). A coordinate bond is drawn as an arrow ($\rightarrow$) pointing directly from the donor atom to the acceptor atom.

Part 4: Comparative Properties (ICSE Favorites)

You are virtually guaranteed to see a 2-3 mark question comparing the physical properties of these compounds. Do not just memorize this table; you must understand the deep reasoning behind each property, as ICSE papers test your conceptual logic rather than your memory.

Property Electrovalent (Ionic) Compounds Covalent Compounds
1. State of Existence Hard, crystalline solids.

The Why:Strong electrostatic forces lock the oppositely charged ions tightly into a rigid, non-moving 3D crystal lattice.		 Usually gases, liquids, or soft solids.

The Why:Molecules are separate and held together by very weak intermolecular forces (Van der Waals forces).
2. Melting & Boiling Points Extremely High.

The Why:Immense amounts of thermal energy (heat) are required to overpower and break the strong electrostatic bonds between the millions of clustered ions.		 Very Low.

The Why:Very little heat is needed because you are only breaking the weak Van der Waals forces between molecules, not breaking the atoms themselves apart.
3. Electrical Conductivity Non-conductors in solid state.
Excellent conductors in molten (melted) or aqueous (dissolved) state.

The Why:Electricity requires mobile charges. In solids, ions are trapped. When melted or dissolved, the lattice breaks and ions become free to move and carry charge.		 Complete Non-conductors.

The Why:They contain neutral molecules, not ions. Without ions, no charge can be carried.

*Critical Exception: Polar covalent compounds (like HCl gas) ionize into $H^+$ and $Cl^-$ when dissolved in water, allowing them to conduct!
4. Solubility Highly soluble in water (polar solvents). Insoluble in organic solvents (like benzene, ether).

The Why:Water is polar. Its positive and negative poles latch onto the ions and literally pull the crystal apart.		 Insoluble in water. Highly soluble in organic (non-polar) solvents.

The Why:Follows the universal chemistry rule: "Like dissolves like." Non-polar molecules easily mix with other non-polar solvents.
ICSE 2019

Past Year Question

Solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity, but aqueous sodium chloride is a good conductor. Explain why logically. [2 Marks]

Show Answer
In the solid state, the $Na^+$ and $Cl^-$ ions are held tightly together by exceptionally strong electrostatic forces of attraction within a rigid, 3D crystal lattice. Because they are locked in place, there are no free, mobile ions to carry the electric current.

However, when dissolved in water (aqueous state), the polar nature of the water molecules severely weakens these electrostatic forces, causing the lattice structure to break apart. The ions separate and become completely free to move throughout the solution, thereby allowing the aqueous solution to conduct electricity perfectly.

Quick Check: Properties

1. Why does boiling water (a covalent compound) only require 100°C, while melting table salt (an ionic compound) requires over 800°C?

View Answer
Boiling water only requires enough heat to break the weak intermolecular forces (Van der Waals forces) holding separate water molecules together. Melting salt requires a massive amount of thermal energy to physically break the immense electrostatic magnetic-like forces binding the millions of sodium and chloride ions together in a crystal structure.

2. True or False: All covalent compounds are completely incapable of conducting electricity in any state.

View Answer
False. While mostly true for non-polar covalent compounds, polar covalent compounds (like Hydrogen Chloride gas) undergo ionization when dissolved in water. They react with water to form free ions ($H_3O^+$ and $Cl^-$), which then act as excellent conductors of electricity.
